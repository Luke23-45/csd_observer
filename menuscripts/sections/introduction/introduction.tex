\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Critical transitions are often preceded by changes in the observable dynamics
of a system. The practical question is not only whether a transition can be
recognized after it occurs, but whether a warning signal can be extracted early
enough to support intervention. This makes early-warning detection a problem of
timing, calibration, and false-alarm control, not only binary classification.

Many early-warning studies evaluate a method on a narrow family of dynamics.
That setting can obscure a basic difficulty: different bifurcations do not
produce the same warning signature. A fold transition, a Hopf transition, and a
logistic transition place different demands on an observer. A method that
tracks one regime well may be poorly calibrated, late, or overly sensitive in
another. In learned observers, the architecture and loss function impose an
inductive bias. That bias can help when it matches the dynamics, but it should
not be assumed to transfer uniformly.

This paper studies that transfer question for learned Kalman observers. We
compare a binary-cross-entropy Kalman observer with a spectral recurrent
variant across synthetic fold, Hopf, and logistic systems. The benchmark varies
the number of training trajectories from 100 to 500 patients and evaluates each
setting across ten random seeds. Performance is measured by detection time,
early-warning AUC, and false-positive rate, so the comparison reflects both
ranking quality and operational calibration.

The main result is regime dependence. The spectral recurrent observer improves
early-warning AUC on fold and Hopf systems at 500 patients and matches the BCE
observer on logistic dynamics, but those gains do not imply uniform dominance
across false-positive control or detection-time summaries. Across patient
counts, the relative behavior of the two observers remains tied to the system
and the metric being optimized. The contribution is therefore not a universal
detector, but a controlled account of where learned observer variants help,
where they trade one objective for another, and why early-warning model
selection should be tied to the dynamical regime.

\subsection{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:background}

Classical early-warning signals are usually built from local changes in a time
series, such as variance or autocorrelation. These summaries are attractive
because they are simple and interpretable, but they also compress the data into
fixed statistics. Observer-based methods take a different view. They estimate a
latent state or warning score over time, allowing the score trajectory to be
learned from data rather than fixed by a hand-designed statistic.

Kalman-style observers combine sequential filtering with explicit state
dynamics. A learned Kalman observer can shape the mapping from measurements to
warning scores while retaining that filtering structure. Spectral
regularization adds a further bias by constraining the learned observer
dynamics. This bias may favor systems where the approach to transition is
expressed through oscillatory or stability-related structure. It may offer less
benefit when the warning signal is better captured by simpler statistics or a
different thresholding behavior.

\subsection{Problem Gap}
\label{sec:gap}

The missing question is not whether a learned observer can perform well on one
system. The harder question is whether the same observer behavior remains
effective across qualitatively different bifurcation mechanisms. A single
aggregate score can hide this distinction. Higher early-warning AUC does not
necessarily imply earlier detection, and earlier detection can come with more
false alarms. Likewise, a method can perform well at one patient count and lose
that advantage when the amount of data changes.

This study addresses the gap by evaluating the same observer variants across
three systems, five patient counts, and three metrics. The goal is to expose the
trade-offs directly rather than collapse them into a single claim of overall
superiority.

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

We make four contributions.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item We define a controlled benchmark for learned Kalman observers across
        fold, Hopf, and logistic transition systems.
  \item We evaluate observer behavior across patient-count settings from 100
        to 500 trajectories, using ten random seeds per setting.
  \item We compare the observers using detection time, early-warning AUC, and
        false-positive rate, rather than relying on a single aggregate metric.
  \item We show that the observed performance is system- and metric-dependent:
        the spectral recurrent observer improves some early-warning AUC
        summaries, but it does not dominate the BCE observer across all systems,
        patient counts, and metrics.
\end{itemize}

These contributions support a narrower and more defensible claim: learned
observer design for early-warning detection should be evaluated as a
regime-dependent modeling problem, not as a search for a detector that is
uniformly best across bifurcation mechanisms.
