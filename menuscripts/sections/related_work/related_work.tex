\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

This work connects classical early-warning indicators, sequential state
observers, and learned models for dynamical systems. The central question is
not which family to prefer in isolation, but how their structural assumptions
interact with the bifurcation mechanism being monitored.

\subsection{Classical Early-Warning Signals}
\label{sec:related:classical}

Classical early-warning analysis rests on the observation that a system
approaching a critical transition recovers more slowly from perturbations.
This critical slowing down motivates statistics such as rising variance and
increasing lag-1 autocorrelation, both of which can grow as the dominant
eigenvalue approaches zero
\citep{scheffer2009early,dakos2008slowing,carpenter2006rising}. These
indicators require no model fitting and are interpretable by construction.

Their limitation is specificity. Each statistic encodes a fixed functional
form for the warning signature. That form may match one bifurcation mechanism
and miss another. Autocorrelation, variance, and related summaries can
therefore be strong on specific transitions without being reliable across all
of them \citep{boettiger2012early,lenton2011early}. This motivates the
multi-metric evaluation used in the present study: a single aggregate score
can hide trade-offs that become visible only when detection time,
discrimination, and false-positive rate are reported separately.

\subsection{Observer and Kalman-Based Approaches}
\label{sec:related:observer}

Kalman filtering provides a sequential framework for estimating latent state
from noisy observations \citep{kalman1960new}. Luenberger observers
generalize this structure to deterministic state estimation with explicit gain
design \citep{luenberger1971introduction}. For early-warning detection, the
sequential form is useful because the warning score at time~\(t\) is shaped
by the full history of observations, not only the current sample.

The learned observer setting retains this sequential structure but treats the
transition, observation, and gain matrices as trainable parameters. This
creates a trade-off absent from classical indicators: the learned mapping can
adapt to a regime-specific warning pattern, but the architecture and loss
function impose an inductive bias that may not transfer. Spectral
regularization is one such bias. By constraining the spectral radius of the
closed-loop dynamics, it favors stable observer trajectories---helpful when
the approach to bifurcation involves progressive loss of stability, less so
when the warning signal has a different structure.

\subsection{Learning Across Dynamical Regimes}
\label{sec:related:regimes}

Recent work has shown that learned models can extract early-warning
information from simulated dynamical systems and, in some cases, transfer to
systems with matching normal-form structure
\citep{bury2021deep,deb2022machine}. These results motivate learned
early-warning detectors but also raise the question that drives the present
study: which aspects of the learned behavior generalize, and which remain
tied to the generating dynamics?

Bifurcation theory provides a structural reason to expect conditional
performance. Fold, Hopf, and period-doubling transitions arise from different
local mechanisms \citep{kuznetsov2004elements,strogatz2015nonlinear} and
produce different pre-transition signatures in the observable time series. A
method tuned to one signature may be poorly calibrated for another. A
cross-bifurcation benchmark is therefore needed to distinguish a useful
regime-specific inductive bias from a detector that transfers uniformly. The
experiments in this paper are designed for that distinction.
