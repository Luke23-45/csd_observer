\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

This work sits between classical early-warning indicators, sequential
observers, and learned models for dynamical systems. The relevant issue is not
which family should be preferred in isolation. The issue is how their
assumptions interact with the bifurcation mechanism being monitored.

\subsection{Classical Early-Warning Signals}
\label{sec:related:classical}

Classical early-warning analysis is built on the idea that a system approaching
a critical transition may recover more slowly from perturbations. This
``critical slowing down'' motivates statistics such as variance and
autocorrelation, which can increase as the dominant return rate weakens
\citep{dakos2008slowing,scheffer2009early,carpenter2006rising}. These indicators are attractive
because they are transparent and require little modeling machinery.

Their simplicity is also a limitation. A fixed statistic assumes that the
warning signature has a known form. That assumption may be reasonable for one
transition mechanism and weak for another. Autocorrelation, variance, and
lag-based summaries can therefore be strong baselines without being complete
detectors \citep{boettiger2012early,lenton2011early}. In this paper they motivate the evaluation standard: learned
observers should be compared against simple signals that already encode useful
dynamical information.

\subsection{Observer and Kalman-Based Approaches}
\label{sec:related:observer}

Kalman filtering provides a sequential framework for estimating latent state
variables from noisy observations \citep{kalman1960new,luenberger1971introduction}. For early-warning
detection, this structure is useful because the warning score is not only a
function of the current sample; it is shaped by the trajectory of observations
leading up to the transition. A Kalman-style observer can smooth noisy
measurements, retain state, and produce a time-indexed score.

The learned observer setting keeps the sequential structure but learns parts of
the mapping from data. This creates a different trade-off from classical
indicators. A learned observer may adapt to a regime-specific warning pattern,
but its architecture and objective also introduce bias. Spectral regularization
is one such bias: it constrains the learned observer dynamics and can be useful
when stability or oscillatory structure is central to the warning signal. The
same constraint need not help when the relevant signal is better captured by a
different statistic or threshold behavior.

\subsection{Learning Across Dynamical Regimes}
\label{sec:related:regimes}

Recent work has shown that learned models can extract early-warning information
from simulated dynamical systems and transfer that information to other
settings when the training data encode suitable normal-form structure
\citep{bury2021deep,deb2022machine}. This line of work motivates learned early-warning
detectors, but it also raises the central question for the present study: which
parts of the learned behavior transfer, and which remain tied to the generating
dynamics?

Bifurcation theory gives a reason to expect conditional performance rather than
uniform dominance. Fold, Hopf, and period-doubling transitions represent
different local mechanisms \citep{kuznetsov2004elements,strogatz2015nonlinear}.
They can therefore favor different warning statistics, score trajectories, and
thresholds. A cross-bifurcation benchmark is needed to separate a useful
regime-specific inductive bias from a detector that transfers uniformly. The
experiments in this paper are designed for that distinction.
