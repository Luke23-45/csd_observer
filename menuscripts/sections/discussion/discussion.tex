\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Regime Dependence Is the Main Finding}
\label{sec:discussion:regime}

The benchmark results show that observer performance is tied to the dynamical
system. Fold and Hopf dynamics produce different pre-transition signatures---a
gradual loss of stability versus an oscillatory onset---and the two observer
variants respond to these differences. On both systems, LSTM-Spec achieves
higher EW-AUC, but on fold the BCE observer detects earlier. On logistic
dynamics, the transition signal is strong enough that both methods discriminate
perfectly; the remaining difference is false-positive rate.

This pattern follows from the benchmark design. The three systems were chosen to
span different bifurcation types, not to be interchangeable. The observers
differ in inductive bias, so a shift in ranking across systems is not a
failure---it is the intended measurement.

\subsection{Why Recurrent and Spectral Structure Helps in Some Settings}
\label{sec:discussion:spectral}

LSTM-Spec adds two components relative to BCE: recurrent aggregation through
the LSTM cell, and a spectral-radius penalty on the closed-loop transition
matrix. Both impose a bias toward detecting gradual changes in the underlying
dynamics.

On fold and Hopf systems, the approach to bifurcation involves a progressive
shift in the effective dynamics. The LSTM aggregates information across time
steps, and the spectral constraint keeps the observer dynamics well-conditioned.
This combination produces sharper score transitions and better signal--null
separation, as reflected in the EW-AUC figures.

On the logistic system, the period-doubling transition is detectable from local
properties of the time series. The additional complexity of LSTM-Spec does not
improve discrimination---both methods reach EW-AUC~\(= 1.0\)---and results in a
higher false-positive rate. The richer scoring head responds to variation in
null trajectories that the simpler BCE head ignores.

\subsection{The BCE Observer Is Not a Weak Baseline}
\label{sec:discussion:baselines}

Kalman-BCE shares the same learned prediction--correction core as LSTM-Spec. It
is a trained observer, not a classical statistic. Its feedforward head produces
scores with a gradual rise profile that, on fold trajectories, crosses the
decision threshold earlier than the sharper LSTM-Spec curve. This gives BCE
higher mean detection time on fold despite lower EW-AUC.

The comparison between two trained observers with the same core architecture is
more informative than a comparison between a trained method and a fixed
statistic. Both methods operate in the same architectural family; the
performance differences are due to the scoring head and regularization, not a
disparity in modeling capacity.

\subsection{Practical Implications}
\label{sec:discussion:implications}

Observer selection for early-warning detection should account for four factors:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \emph{Dynamical regime.} LSTM-Spec is preferable when the transition
        involves gradual dynamical changes (fold, Hopf). BCE matches or exceeds
        LSTM-Spec on systems where the transition signal is locally strong
        (logistic).
  \item \emph{False-positive tolerance.} If the false-positive rate must be
        minimized, BCE is the safer choice on fold and logistic. On Hopf, both
        methods have comparably low FPR at sufficient patient counts.
  \item \emph{Warning lead time.} BCE tends to produce earlier threshold
        crossings, but with higher variance. LSTM-Spec produces later, more
        consistent crossings.
  \item \emph{Data availability.} LSTM-Spec benefits more from additional
        training trajectories on fold and Hopf. On logistic, both methods
        saturate by 200~patients.
\end{itemize}

These factors trade off against each other. The benchmark provides the data to
make that trade-off explicit for each system, rather than collapsing it into a
single ranking.
